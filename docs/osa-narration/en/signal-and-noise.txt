Lesson 4 of 8.

Learning objectives. Distinguish primary sources, summaries and independent corroboration. Identify publications that depend on the same original source. Treat uncertainty as a valid conclusion when evidence is limited.

More publications do not mean more evidence. Several pieces may cite the same original statement, summarise one another or use one dataset. There appears to be more information, but the basis for the claim has not necessarily changed. Tracing provenance helps distinguish independent confirmation from repetition.

Assess the quality of the evidence. After finding a source, examine who produced it, when it was published, how its data were obtained and what limitations it states. A source can be primary yet incomplete or unsuitable for the question. The claim must fit the evidence in meaning, time and scope.

Unknown is a valid conclusion. When evidence is limited or sources disagree, do not force a confident choice. State what is known, what is disputed and what fact could clarify the question. This makes research reproducible and shows where further work is needed.

Practical example. Three websites may repeat one press release. There are more pages, but not necessarily three independent pieces of evidence. Follow citations back to the original document and check whether it supports the claim.

Key terms. Primary source. The original document, dataset, method or statement on which a claim is based. Independent corroboration. Evidence checked separately rather than copied from the same source.

Practical exercise. Trace the origin of a claim. Choose a news item or claim repeated across several websites. Follow citations and locate the earliest available primary document. Compare dates, wording, evidence and limitations. Mark which reports are independent, repeat one another or offer no evidence. Success criteria. You can describe where the claim originated and what independent evidence exists without counting copies as separate proof.

Key takeaways. Repeating one source does not create independent corroboration. A primary document still needs methodological and contextual review. “Unknown” is better than unsupported certainty.
