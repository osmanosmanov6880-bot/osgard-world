Lesson 1 of 8.

Learning objectives. Distinguish a useful AI output from evidence that it is true. Choose a task with criteria that can be defined before using a model. Match human review to the risk of an error.

A useful answer is not proof. An AI system can produce a quick draft, explain a term or suggest alternatives. Those can be useful actions, but they do not establish that every claim is true. Separate presentation quality from evidence quality: fluent, courteous prose may still contain errors.

Define the task before choosing the tool. Start with the desired outcome and constraints. A small task with a clear criterion is usually a better first experiment than an open-ended request to “do everything”. Split larger work into parts and retain human review wherever an error could change the outcome.

Measure results, not impressions. To learn whether a tool helps, compare its output with the usual workflow against the same criteria. Count review time, corrections, privacy concerns and the cost of mistakes. One successful example is a starting point for evaluation, not universal proof of effectiveness.

Practical example. Ask a model to explain an unfamiliar topic. Use the explanation as a first-pass map, then pick one consequential claim and check it against a primary source. Do not judge the output only by its confident tone or polished writing.

Key terms. Quality criterion. A condition defined in advance for evaluating a result, such as accuracy, completeness, clarity or suitability for the task. Verifiable claim. A statement that can be compared with data, a document, an observation or a reliable source.

Practical exercise. Compare a workflow with and without AI. Choose a small, repeatable, low-risk task. Record the usual completion time and define what a good result means. Ask AI for a draft, then verify facts and correct errors. Compare time, quality, edits and new risks with the original workflow. Success criteria. Record a concrete comparison and its limitations. Do not generalise about AI from one example.

Key takeaways. Fluent text does not guarantee correctness. Set quality criteria before generation. Base the final judgement on a result that can be checked.
